Software grows faster than hardware improves

pc replacement cycles. then vs now

How long a computer was expected to last

Era Avg. Replacement Cycle Context Source
1980s 10+ years IBM PC ran the same software for a decade with zero degradation. Obsolescence was physical. iFixit
Early 1990s 7–8 years Enterprise IT departments standardized 7-year refresh cycles. Hardware outpaced software demands. iFixit
Early 2000s 4 years Windows XP drove upgrade pressure. Intel CEO later confirmed: "Four years was the average." PCWorld / Krzanich
2016 5–6 years Cycles briefly lengthened as hardware improvement slowed, not because devices lasted longer, but because the next device wasn't meaningfully faster. PCWorld / Krzanich
2024 3.7 years Windows 11 hardware requirements, AI feature gating, and planned obsolescence drive replacement even as hardware improvement slows. Gartner via Sobrii
The 2016 blip where cycles lengthened is telling: hardware stopped improving fast enough to justify replacing. By 2024 they shortened again. not because hardware got better, but because software policy made old hardware incompatible. (PCWorld, Sobrii/Gartner)

consequence 01. then vs now
Then. 1981 to ~2000
  • An IBM PC ran the same software for 10+ years with zero performance degradation (Computer History Museum)
  • Obsolescence was physical. The hardware literally wore out or became too slow for new tasks
  • No software update could make a working machine slower
  • Average device lifespan: 7–10 years (iFixit)
Now. 2010 to present
  • A working iPhone was deliberately slowed via software update. Apple admitted it, settled for $500M (Fortune)
  • Obsolescence is engineered. Support windows are 3–5 years by design, not by physics (Gartner via Sobrii)
  • Average device lifespan today: 3–4 years (iFixit)
  • The device still works. The company decided otherwise. (Fortune)
consequence 01. who decides
Ending Windows 10 support October 2025, forcing hardware replacement for hundreds of millions of PCs that still run perfectly. (Microsoft)
Drops iOS support after 5–6 years. A 2018 iPhone in perfect condition gets no security updates in 2024, making it functionally unusable for banking apps, payments, and secure login. (Macworld)
Android security updates end after 3 years. Chromebook auto-update expiry: 6–8 years from manufacture. A school district's 2019 Chromebooks were unsupported by 2024. (Google)
Logos: Microsoft (Wikimedia Commons) · Apple (Wikimedia Commons) · Google (Wikipedia)
consequence 01. reliability then vs now
Then. 1980s to 1990s
  • A PC bought in 1984 ran the same software in 1994 without degradation (Computer History Museum)
  • Hardware was over-engineered. Built to outlast the software it ran
  • IBM PC keyboards were rated for over 100 million keystrokes per key (Smithsonian)
  • Repair was expected, documented, and supported by manufacturers
Now. 2010s to present
  • MacBook butterfly keyboards (2015–2019) were so failure-prone Apple faced a $50M class action and abandoned the design (MacRumors)
  • Devices are deliberately under-engineered for longevity to drive replacement cycles (iFixit)
  • iFixit repairability score: iPhone 14 = 4/10. iPhone 15 = 7/10. (iFixit)
  • Repair is restricted, undocumented, and voided by warranty (FTC, 2021)
consequence 01. the lifespan gap
10 yrs → 3 yrs
Average device lifespan: 1985 baseline vs. 2024 baseline. The device didn't get worse. The definition of “current” got shorter. (iFixit)
$512B
US consumer technology industry revenue in 2024. Up $14 billion from 2023. (Consumer Technology Association)

consequence 02. then vs now
Then. pre-2000
  • An IBM PC purchased in 1981 was still in active office use in 1991 (Computer History Museum)
  • Obsolescence was physical. The hardware genuinely wore out (iFixit)
  • A household might own one computer across an entire decade (Computer History Museum)
  • E-waste wasn't a category. Not enough volume to matter (UNEP)
Now. 2010 to present
  • Average device lifespan: 3–4 years before forced obsolescence (iFixit)
  • 53.6 million tons of e-waste globally in 2019. A record at the time (UNEP)
  • Only 17.4% formally recycled. 82.6% dumped, burned, or illegally exported (UNEP)
consequence 02. where it goes
“The fate of 82.6% (44.3 Mt) of e-waste generated globally in 2019 was most likely not formally collected and recycled in an environmentally sound way.”
UNEP, Global E-waste Monitor 2020 (UNEP)
~40%
Of US e-waste delivered to recyclers was exported rather than recycled domestically (Basel Action Network)

consequence 02. the carbon cost
“The manufacturing of a new laptop generates an average of 331 kg of CO₂ equivalent — before it’s ever turned on.”
Circular Computing (circularcomputing.com)
75–85%
Of a device's total lifetime carbon footprint is generated during manufacturing. Before it's ever turned on. (Circular Computing)
The baseline shift made this exponentially worse.
Then: 1 laptop manufactured per household per decade = ~35kg CO₂/year from manufacturing. (Circular Computing)
Now: 1 laptop every 3–4 years = ~90–130kg CO₂/year from manufacturing alone. (Circular Computing, iFixit)
consequence 02. no federal law
Then. when e-waste was minimal
  • At the old baseline pace, e-waste volume was low enough that no federal law was needed (UNEP)
  • The EU ratified the Basel Convention banning e-waste exports in 1989 (Basel Action Network)
  • The problem was small enough to be invisible to US legislators (EPA)
Now. at the new baseline pace
  • 6.9M tons of e-waste generated in the US per year (EPA)
  • 0 federal e-waste recycling laws. The US never ratified the Basel Convention (EPA)
  • 25 states have no e-waste law at all (EPA)
CONSEQUENCE 03
Policy
Can't Keep Up
consequence 03. then vs now
Then. 1980s baseline
  • CFAA (1986) written for its time. Mainframes, early networks. Legislators understood what they were regulating. (EFF)
  • Consumer electronics covered by product liability laws as durable goods with expected lifespans (iFixit)
  • Repair was legal, expected, and supported by manufacturers (FTC, 2021)
Now. 2025 baseline
  • CFAA still the primary US computer law (EFF)
  • No federal right to repair, no e-waste law, no software transparency requirement (FTC, 2021)
  • Digital rights law consistently lags behind technology — often by a decade or more. (EFF)
consequence 03. right to repair
Then
  • Any device to any repair shop. No restriction (FTC, 2021)
  • Parts standardized and available indefinitely (FTC, 2021)
  • Repair manuals published and public (FTC, 2021)
  • A repaired device legally identical to an unrepaired one (FTC, 2021)
Now
  • Proprietary screws, glue, software-paired parts block repair (FTC, 2021)
  • FTC: “scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications” (FTC, 2021)
  • EU Right to Repair Directive passed 2024. US has nothing equivalent (EU Commission)
consequence 03. government access programs
23M
Households that lost the Affordable Connectivity Program subsidy when Congress failed to renew it in June 2024 (FCC)
77%
Of ACP recipients said losing it would force them to drop service or change to a slower plan (FCC survey)
At the old baseline, internet access wasn't a survival need.
In 1990, not having internet access was normal. In 2026, not having internet access means no job applications, no healthcare portals, no school assignments, no government services.
consequence 03. eu vs us
European Union. acknowledged the shift
  • Right to Repair Directive. 10 years of spare parts, March 2024 (EU Commission)
  • France: mandatory repairability score on all electronics since 2021 (EU Commission)
  • Universal USB-C charging law. Forced Apple to drop Lightning (charging cable) in 2023 (EU Commission)
  • Basel Convention ratified. E-waste exports banned (Basel Action Network)
United States. still at the old baseline
  • No federal right to repair law (FTC, 2021)
  • No federal e-waste law. Basel Convention never ratified (EPA)
  • No mandatory repairability scoring (FTC, 2021)
  • No universal charger requirement (FTC, 2021)
  • CFAA still the primary computer law. Written in 1986 (EFF)
CONSEQUENCE 04
The Gap
Widens
consequence 04. the opt-out penalty
Then. 1985 baseline
  • A business with no computer competed on equal terms. product, service, location (US Chamber of Commerce)
  • Job applicants mailed resumes (US Chamber of Commerce)
  • Students used the library (Pew Research)
  • Non-participation in technology was a neutral choice (US Chamber of Commerce)
Now. 2025 baseline
  • A business without a website, payment system, and social media is invisible (US Chamber of Commerce)
  • Most job applications require online account and 24-hour email response (Pew Research)
  • Tests, college apps, and homework are online-only (Pew Research)
  • Non-participation is now a penalty. Even for people who didn't choose to participate (Pew Research)
consequence 04. small business
1990: A local bookstore competes with other local bookstores. The baseline for competition is inventory, location, and service. (US Chamber of Commerce)
2025: That same bookstore now competes with Amazon's recommendation algorithm, same-day delivery, and a catalog of 300 million items. To survive, it needs a website, an inventory management system, an email list, a social media presence, and an e-commerce platform. (US Chamber of Commerce)
The baseline for what it means to “compete” moved. The traditional business owner didn't opt into that shift. it happened to them. (Pew Research)
67
Q1 2026 Small Business Index score — down from 72.0 at its recent high, as inflation and economic uncertainty weigh on confidence (US Chamber of Commerce)
$0
Cost to not have a website in 1990. Today, no web presence means near-zero discoverability for most consumer businesses. (US Chamber of Commerce)

Beginning in the mid-1990s, coordinated industry roadmaps converted Moore’s Law from an empirical observation into a self-fulfilling prophecy — the industry collectively decided the pace of change. (Flamm, NBER 2018)
“The industry controls what you think you need.” (Morrow)

WHERE DOES
THIS LEAVE US
reflecting on the argument
Original Argument

What are the consequences of a computing industry that advances faster than the consumers it serves?

Finding

Consumers got genuine value and lost their ability to evaluate the industry simultaneously. The baseline shifted so far that the question of whether it was good or bad is almost impossible to answer from inside it.

solutions

What would change the situation

01
Hardware Longevity Standards
Mandatory minimum support periods. EU requires 10 years of spare parts. (EU Right to Repair Directive)
02
Tech Literacy in Schools
Teaching hardware history gives students the reference point that generational amnesia denies them. (Suzuki Foundation)
03
Right to Repair
FTC called manufacturer restrictions anticompetitive in 2021. Repair access disrupts the upgrade treadmill. (FTC. Nixing the Fix)
04
Software Transparency
Require software to disclose actual vs. recommended specs. Make Wirth's Law visible so consumers can push back. (Wikipedia. Wirth's Law)
05
Digital Equity Programs
Long-term funding for access programs. Ending the ACP cut off 23M households in June 2024. (FCC)
